Overview

Abstract

Precise cloth manipulation is difficult to learn from a small set of successful demonstrations: a policy may approach the cloth while still missing the corners required for a stable bimanual grasp. We study a staged alternative. An ACT policy is first trained on 100 direct corner-grasp demonstrations using a blue square towel. The policy is then deployed, and a human operator takes over at states where the grippers are misaligned or likely to miss. These interventions produce 101 full-task trajectories on red and green towels. Fine-tuning from the corner-grasp checkpoint reaches 70% full-task success over 50 random-placement trials, compared with 26% for the pretrained policy and 20% for a policy trained from scratch on the HIL data. The results support corrective HIL fine-tuning as a practical route for this system, while leaving position-controlled robustness and cross-instance generalization open.

Method

A curriculum defined by policy failures

01

Learn the prerequisite

Train base20k for 20k steps on 100 blue-square demonstrations of direct corner grasping.

02

Visit failure states

Run the policy on the robot. Misalignment and near-miss states are reached by the policy rather than scripted in advance.

03

Correct and finish

The operator takes control with two leader arms, recovers the grasp and completes lifting and folding. This yields 101 HIL episodes.

04

Fine-tune the full task

Continue from base20k for 10k HIL steps. Compare against a 20k-step scratch model trained on the same HIL data.

Scope

What the experiment does not establish

  • All models use one training seed and one robot/camera configuration.
  • Random placement was not logged as position or orientation, so placement difficulty cannot be balanced or stratified after the fact.
  • Only binary condition-level outcomes were retained; stage failures, task time and fold error are unavailable.
  • The white towel changes color, instance and geometry together. Its 4/10 result is not a shape-only ablation.
  • The custom fingers, corrective-state sampling and full-task labels were not isolated in separate ablations.

Within these limits, the result is clear: preserving the prerequisite corner-grasp skill and fine-tuning on corrective full-task trajectories is substantially more effective than either control used in this study.
